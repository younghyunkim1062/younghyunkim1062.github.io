What my advisor taught me

From Bench to Paper

A bioengineering grad-school roadmap — the flow I learned, step by step

Grad school produced two kinds of writing for me — an experimental thesis and a first-author review paper. They grow from the same foundation but need different muscles, so I've split the path in two. Work through the shared foundation first, then follow whichever track you're on. The links and tools along the way are the shortcuts I wish someone had handed me on day one.

Same foundation, two kinds of paper — two different muscles.

Foundations

Where everyone starts

The habits and basics that both tracks are built on.

  • Every experiment runs the same loop: do it, organize the data cleanly, then document the whole thing in slides. Get that order into your bones early — it's the backbone of everything else here.
  • Slides and a lab notebook aren't decoration; they're your lab's memory. Keep a notebook from day one, and treat no experiment as finished until it's a recorded deck.
  • Sterile technique and basic cell culture come before anything precious — prove you can keep a simple culture clean for weeks before you touch hiPSCs.
  • Shadow a senior student through an entire protocol, start to finish, before you run it solo.

Our lab's real work is human iPSC-derived cardiac tissue — but you don't start there. You build the fundamentals on a forgiving cell line first, then graduate to stem cells once the basics are automatic.

Step 1 — Build the basics on C2C12. It's cheap, robust, and forgiving, which makes it the right place to learn sterile technique before you touch anything precious:

  • Learn what C2C12 cells are — where they come from and what they differentiate into.
    ↗ ATCC · C2C12 (CRL-1772)
  • Read the C2C12 protocol end to end, then re-read the culture guide until the four core moves feel automatic — thawing/seeding, passaging, differentiation, and freezing.
    ↗ ATCC Culture Guides

Step 2 — Graduate to human iPSCs (our real focus). Once the fundamentals are second nature, step up to the cells the lab actually publishes on:

  • Learn feeder-free hiPSC maintenance — hiPSCs are far more sensitive than C2C12, so everything you drilled on the easy line now matters twice as much.
  • Understand directed cardiac differentiation, then chamber-specific identity — ventricular vs. atrial (e.g. markers like NR2F2 / IRX4, and cTNT / TNNT2 for cardiomyocyte purity).

Experimental research

Generating your own data and turning it into a thesis.

  • Confirm your differentiation with the right assays — flow cytometry, qRT-PCR, and immunofluorescence — not just how the cells look.
  • This workflow is how I confirmed ~97.5% cTNT⁺ ventricular differentiation and chamber-specific identity (NR2F2 / IRX4). → See Research
  • Start with ImageJ for quick measurements. When the existing tools don't fit your data, build your own — and always validate it against ground truth before you trust it.
  • When ImageJ wasn't enough, I built iCAMAnalyzer for calcium-imaging analysis and validated it to ~7% error against synthetic ground truth; I also wrote Streamlit multi-organoid pipelines and a diameter analyzer checked with Bland–Altman. → See Research
  • Turn raw data into figures with one clear message each — redraw plots rather than pasting raw software output.
  • I design my own paper figures, from data plots to graphical abstracts. → See Figures & Illustration
  • Pull the whole arc together into a written work. Write your methods as you run the experiments, not six months later.
  • For me that was my master's thesis on hiPSC-derived chamber-specific cardiac organoids — and a standalone manuscript is the natural next step.

Review & synthesis

A different muscle: not generating data, but making sense of a whole field.

  • Choose a scope narrow enough to say something new — not "everything about X."
  • A review needs an argument, not just a summary. Decide early what your review is really claiming about where the field is and where it's going.
  • The running-document habit from the Foundation pays off here: every paper with a one-line takeaway slowly becomes your outline.
  • Group papers by theme, not just chronology — the groupings are the skeleton of your sections.
  • A strong review is anchored by a single conceptual figure that captures the whole field at a glance. This is where figure-design skill matters most.
  • Sketch it early — it forces you to decide what the review is actually organized around. → See Figures & Illustration
  • Outline → section skeletons → prose. Expect many rounds of revision.
  • This is exactly how I approached my first-author review integrating organoids, engineered tissues, and microphysiological systems. → See Publications
