Research · Cardiac tissue engineering & quantitative physiology

Connecting cardiac identity with calcium dynamics and contraction.

I build human cardiac organoid models and develop tools to measure how they function. My work brings together stem-cell culture, imaging, and quantitative analysis to ask how atrial- and ventricular-like tissues differ—and what those measurements can actually tell us.

Completed M.S. work

Do chamber-associated identities produce distinct functional phenotypes?

I developed atrial- and ventricular-like cardiac organoids using Wnt and retinoic-acid modulation, with optimization of cell seeding and aggregation. The workflow begins with hiPSC culture in 2D; embryoid-body formation marks the transition to 3D, followed by cardiac differentiation in 3D.

I used qRT-PCR to assess chamber-associated markers and flow cytometry to assess cardiomyocyte identity in monolayer cultures, alongside immunofluorescence in 3D tissues. These measurements supported tissue characterization; functional imaging then tested whether the groups also differed in calcium dynamics and contraction.

Software integration & synthetic validation completed

iCAMAnalyzer

An integrated Python platform for calcium-transient analysis, calcium-based activation mapping, and label-free tissue-motion analysis.

Calcium fluorescence and tissue motion were measured separately, making it difficult to examine complementary readouts within a common workflow. I developed iCAMAnalyzer to bring their analysis into one Python platform, integrating earlier modules and testing how acquisition and processing choices affect the measurements. Software integration made the analyses more coherent; it did not make the original recordings simultaneous.

01 · Calcium kinetics

How does a beat evolve?

Fluorescence traces describe transient timing and duration. Beat detection adapts to estimated beating frequency to support comparison across recordings.

02 · Calcium propagation

How does activation spread?

Pixelwise timing from the calcium upstroke produces calcium-based activation maps. Local polynomial fitting estimates calcium-derived propagation velocity and direction.

03 · Tissue motion

How does tissue move?

Label-free brightfield analysis characterizes beat timing and contraction duration. Motion provides a complementary readout of tissue function.

Measurement scope: Calcium-derived propagation velocity describes the spread of the calcium signal. It is not a direct measurement of electrical conduction velocity.

Validating the measurement itself

I generated synthetic videos with known propagation velocities and evaluated the effects of frame rate, noise, and spatial resolution. I also checked activation estimates against experimental videos frame by frame.

This work helped identify when an apparent functional difference might instead reflect the measurement process. I additionally tested the motion workflow on organoid samples from a collaborating group at Konkuk University.

M.S. observations

Functional differences were readout-dependent.

Known differences in atrial and ventricular action potentials motivated the comparison. Here, I measured calcium transients and tissue motion; these recordings do not directly measure action-potential waveforms.

Calcium kinetics

Atrial-like organoids showed faster, shorter calcium transients, while ventricular-like organoids showed more prolonged transients in the evaluated recordings.

Tissue motion

Beat rate and contraction duration also differed between the groups, providing complementary functional information.

Calcium-derived propagation

I generated calcium-based activation maps but have not identified a clear atrial–ventricular difference in calcium-derived propagation velocity. This observation does not establish that the groups are equivalent.

These observations motivated a closer look at the relationship between calcium dynamics and contraction. Separate recordings can describe each phenotype, but resolving their timing within the same beat requires synchronized acquisition.

Hardware in development

Synchronized calcium–brightfield imaging

I am developing a dual-camera microscope that uses dichroic wavelength separation to acquire Fluo-4 calcium fluorescence and brightfield motion simultaneously. Paired recordings will let me ask how the timing and duration of a calcium transient relate to motion within the same beat.

Hardware integration and timing validation are ongoing. Calcium–motion imaging addresses the link between calcium dynamics and contraction; resolving the full electrical–calcium–mechanical sequence will additionally require electrical recording.

Future research direction

When does the relationship between activation and contraction change?

I want to use synchronized measurements to study how chamber identity, mechanical loading, and disease-associated perturbations change the relationship between electrical activity, calcium handling, and contraction.

A longer-term question is whether tissue motion contains enough information to estimate selected features of calcium dynamics. I view this as a hypothesis to test against paired measurements, with explicit limits on what can be inferred.

Related work in development includes geometry-based simulations, a system combining electrical stimulation with mechanical stretch, and integration with microelectrode-array measurements. These tools are intended to test how controlled perturbations alter cardiac function.
