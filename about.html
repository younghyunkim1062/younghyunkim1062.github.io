About Me

I'm Young Hyun Kim, a bioengineering researcher interested in building human cardiac tissues and developing quantitative tools to understand how they function.

My path into bioengineering did not begin at the bench. Before working with stem cells, I built interactive robotics projects, developed STEAM education programs, and taught students and teachers how to turn ideas into working systems. That same instinct—to build, test, and understand—eventually led me from machines to living tissues.

Today, my work combines stem-cell biology, tissue engineering, quantitative imaging, computation, and instrumentation.

Moved from machines to living systems

Joined a newly forming tissue-engineering research group at Sejong University and began working with engineered muscle and cardiac tissues.

Became a tissue engineer

Completed my B.S. and began my M.S. in the Biological Systems Engineering Laboratory, focusing on human iPSC-derived cardiac tissue engineering.

2024

Learned to measure what I built

As my cardiac organoid work expanded, I began developing custom computational tools for quantitative analysis of tissue morphology, calcium dynamics, contraction, and electrical propagation.

2025

Expanded from analysis to instrumentation

Extended my engineering work into bioinstrumentation and simulation, including an electromechanical stimulation platform, 3D-printed tissue systems, and COMSOL-guided design.

Co-authored a review on biohybrid actuators in npj Robotics.

2026

Built the tissue and the tools

Completed my M.S. research on chamber-specific cardiac organoids and developed iCAMAnalyzer for quantitative cardiac functional analysis. Published my first-author review in Microsystems & Nanoengineering and began preparing for Ph.D. training.

Build the tissue. Measure the function. Ask the next question.

2027 →

Next chapter: Ph.D. training

I hope to extend cardiac tissue engineering and quantitative bioimaging toward mechanistic models of cardiac disease, particularly chamber-specific electromechanical dysfunction.

Coming full circle

What Ties It Together

Across robotics, teaching, tissue engineering, and computational analysis, the common thread in my work has been building systems and finding ways to understand how they behave. Today, that means engineering human cardiac tissues and developing the tools needed to quantify their function. For my Ph.D., I hope to carry that approach into mechanistic models of cardiac disease.
