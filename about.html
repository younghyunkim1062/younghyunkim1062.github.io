About Me

I'm Young Hyun Kim, a bioengineering researcher interested in building human cardiac tissues and developing quantitative tools to understand how they function.

My path into bioengineering did not begin at the bench. Before working with stem cells, I built interactive robotics projects, developed STEAM education programs, and taught students and teachers how to turn ideas into working systems.

Today, my work combines stem-cell biology, tissue engineering, quantitative imaging, computation, and instrumentation.

2022

Moved from machines to living systems.

Joined a newly forming tissue-engineering research group and began working with engineered skeletal muscle and cardiac tissues.

2023–2024

Began building and measuring cardiac tissues.

Started my M.S. research on hiPSC-derived cardiac organoids and began developing quantitative tools for morphology, calcium dynamics, and contraction.

2025

Expanded from analysis to instrumentation.

Extended my work into COMSOL-guided design, 3D-printed tissue systems, and electromechanical stimulation; co-authored a review in npj Robotics.

2026

Built the tissue and the tools.

Completed my M.S. research on chamber-specific cardiac organoids, developed iCAMAnalyzer, and published my first-author review in Microsystems & Nanoengineering.

Before the Bench

Before I built tissues, I built robots.

CreArtBot project video

From 2017 to 2019, I worked with CreArtBot on robotics and STEAM education using BirdBrain Technologies' Hummingbird platform.

I designed interactive exhibition pieces for conferences, helped develop hands-on STEAM education programs, and taught robotics and creative engineering to both students and teachers.

I also visited BirdBrain Technologies in the United States and participated in hands-on workshops with the team behind the Hummingbird platform.

Those experiences taught me to approach technology as something to build, test, explain, and share—a mindset that later carried naturally into bioengineering.

Coming full circle

What Ties It Together

Building systems—and learning how they behave.

Across robotics, teaching, tissue engineering, and computational analysis, the common thread in my work has been building systems and finding ways to understand how they behave.

What began with interactive robots eventually led me to engineered biological systems. Today, that means building human cardiac tissues and developing quantitative tools to measure their structure and function.

For my Ph.D., I hope to extend this approach toward mechanistic models of cardiac disease, combining tissue engineering with quantitative bioimaging, computation, and bioinstrumentation.

I build cardiac tissues—and the tools to tell whether they work.
